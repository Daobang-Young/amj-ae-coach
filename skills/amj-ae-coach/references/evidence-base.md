## 1. Evidence hierarchy

Use these levels in descending authority:

1. **Current official policy**: live AMJ/AOM submission, style, ethics, and AI pages. Treat explicit requirements as binding on the verification date.
2. **Official AMJ editorial guidance**: high-authority articulation of what editors value, but not a mechanical acceptance formula.
3. **Methodological norms**: discipline standards that may be necessary for a specific design; label `[METHOD-NORM]`.
4. **Coach inference**: diagnosis derived from the manuscript; label `[COACH-INFERENCE]`.

The seven editorials form a connected author-development framework, not a secret AE checklist. Do not turn their examples or historical page heuristics into universal current rules.

## 2. Seven-editorial map

### `[AMJ-P1]` Topic choice

Colquitt and George (2011), pp. 432–435. Test five properties:

- **Significance**: address a large unresolved problem in a literature and explain the larger puzzle.
- **Novelty**: change the scholarly conversation rather than add a nearby incremental result; recombination across literatures can help.
- **Curiosity**: create a genuine puzzle, mystery, counterintuitive tension, or uncertain ending rather than a foregone conclusion.
- **Scope**: cover the constructs, mechanisms, and perspectives needed for the promised question; avoid thin slicing.
- **Actionability**: produce insight relevant to managerial or organizational practice.

Pinpoints: significance p. 432; novelty and curiosity pp. 433–434; scope and actionability pp. 434–435.

### `[AMJ-P2]` Research design

Bono and McNamara (2011), pp. 657–660.

- Match the design, sample, and procedure to the research question.
- Treat cross-sectional evidence as a poor match for claims about change, temporal process, or strong causality; mediation language carries causal implications.
- Define constructs and boundaries before operationalization; avoid jingle-jangle slippage.
- Justify altered measures and supply validity evidence; do not relabel a proxy as a different construct without support.
- Address common-method threats, theoretically justified controls, omitted-variable risks, and measured mediating mechanisms.
- Consider complementary studies or samples whose weaknesses offset one another.

Pinpoints: question-design fit p. 657; samples and construct validity pp. 658–659; controls, mediators, and multiple studies pp. 659–660.

### `[AMJ-P3]` Setting the hook

Grant and Pollock (2011), pp. 873–879.

- Make the introduction answer: **Who cares? What do we know, not know, and why does it matter? What will we learn?**
- Join and problematize a conversation through a puzzle, controversy, paradox, inadequacy, or important unresolved question—not gap spotting alone.
- Preview how the study changes, challenges, or advances understanding.
- Keep the opening focused and accessible; do not overpromise or introduce too many frameworks.
- Treat the historical three-page and repeated-rewriting advice as craft heuristics, not current submission rules.

Pinpoints: three questions pp. 873–875; writing process pp. 875–876; focus, engagement, and pitfalls pp. 877–878.

### `[AMJ-P4]` Grounding hypotheses

Sparrowe and Mayer (2011), pp. 1098–1102.

- Build a theoretically driven explanatory narrative, not a chronological literature review or argument by citation.
- Make the logic explain why X should relate to Y without forcing readers to retrieve the cited theory.
- Combine established theory, relevant evidence, and a causal/process account; operationalize key mediators when they carry the explanation.
- State boundary conditions and explain why each theory and variable is needed.
- Integrate multiple theories rather than assigning a different theory ad hoc to every link.
- Avoid vague general-theory appeals, fragmented theorizing, and obvious hypotheses for which no plausible null/opposite exists.

Pinpoints: prior research p. 1098; explanatory logic pp. 1099–1100; coherence and pitfalls pp. 1100–1101.

### `[AMJ-P5]` Methods and results

Zhang and Shaw (2012), pp. 8–12. Apply the three C's: **completeness, clarity, credibility**.

- Methods must disclose how, what, and why; provide enough detail to understand and replicate data construction, measures, coding, and analyses.
- Explain sample choice, construct-to-measure fit, operationalization, controls, coding, and analytic/model choices.
- Results should identify unit, sample size, dependent variable, descriptive evidence, hypothesis linkage, and every supported or unsupported result.
- Use plots, simple slopes, alternatives, and robustness checks where needed to justify interpretation.
- Report practical magnitude, not only statistical significance.

Pinpoints: methods pp. 8–10; results pp. 10–12.

### `[AMJ-P6]` Discussing implications

Geletkanycz and Tepper (2012), pp. 256–260.

- Close the loop on the motivating theoretical puzzle and synthesize findings collectively.
- Open a new theoretical beginning by explaining what changes, why, under what conditions, and how it connects to broader literature.
- Use unexpected and unsupported findings carefully as possible theoretical information, without disguising post hoc claims as a priori.
- Focus deeply on two or three central implications.
- Avoid rehashing results, meandering across disconnected implications, and overreaching beyond the data or front-end framing.

Pinpoints: ending/new beginning pp. 256–258; pitfalls pp. 258–260.

### `[AMJ-P7]` Qualitative distinctions

Bansal and Corley (2012), pp. 509–513.

- Most qualitative AMJ work builds theory inductively, though qualitative data can also support deduction.
- Use a shorter multipurpose front end and a longer back end that integrates analyzed data, emergent theory, and target literatures.
- Describe methods comprehensively, personally, reflexively, and transparently enough to show the nonlinear research journey.
- Show—not merely describe—data through disciplined, creative displays that connect raw evidence, analysis, and theory.
- Interweave data and theory narratives into a distinctive, revelatory story; embrace iterative research and writing.

Pinpoints: inductive purpose/front end pp. 509–510; methods and displays pp. 510–511; story and process pp. 511–513.

## 3. Cross-paper synthesis

Use this causal chain when diagnosing a manuscript:

`important problem -> conversation-changing question -> coherent mechanism -> design capable of testing it -> transparent evidence -> bounded theoretical advance`

A downstream writing fix cannot repair an upstream topic or design failure. Conversely, a strong design can still be obscured by an introduction that frames only a gap, a theory section that cites without explaining, results that hide counterevidence, or a discussion that overclaims.

## 4. Interpretation limits

- Do not call every absent citation a fatal flaw; ask whether omission changes positioning or inference.
- Do not treat one design as universally superior. Judge fitness for the claim.
- Do not demand multiple studies mechanically. Ask whether complementary evidence is needed to address the central validity threats.
- Do not equate statistical complexity with contribution or credibility.
- Do not infer novelty from the manuscript's reference list alone.
