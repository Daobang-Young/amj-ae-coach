# Reviewer Logic

## Purpose

Use this reference to prevent formulaic entrepreneurship writing advice from replacing scholarly judgment. Treat it as a red-team protocol, not a prediction of editorial outcomes.

## Readiness gates

Use one gate without percentages:

1. **Outside scope**: the manuscript does not centrally explain an entrepreneurial phenomenon or does not fit the named journal/article type.
2. **Concept/design failure**: the main theoretical or empirical claim cannot be supported by the current logic, operationalization, data, or design; writing cannot repair it.
3. **Promising but not review-ready**: the phenomenon and evidence have potential, but major framing, theory, design, or reporting problems remain.
4. **Ready for expert pre-review/submission preparation**: no known fatal issue remains; residual risks and verification needs are explicit.

Gate 4 is not an acceptance forecast. Recommend review by a knowledgeable human colleague.

## Eight red-team corrections

### 1. Replace field-membership by phenomenon centrality

Do not classify a paper as entrepreneurship research merely because the sample contains entrepreneurs, startups, family firms, SMEs, or self-employed people. Require the entrepreneurial character of the phenomenon to matter to the question, explanation, evidence, or boundary conditions.

### 2. Replace gap filling by conversation change

An unstudied relationship is not a contribution by itself. Identify the consequential puzzle, contradiction, inadequacy, or boundary and state what readers would believe differently if the claim holds.

### 3. Replace the fixed two-literature formula by input-transformation-output

Use a second literature when it creates explanatory leverage. Then specify:

- **Input**: the concepts or mechanism imported or combined.
- **Transformation**: why entrepreneurial uncertainty, action, time pressure, resource constraints, identity, institutions, or process changes the theory's operation.
- **Output**: what the study returns to entrepreneurship scholarship and, when warranted, to the source or broader management theory.

Permit entrepreneurship-native theory building and theory export. Do not require external borrowing when the focal conversation can generate the needed explanation.

### 4. Interpret “barn builder” as disciplined criticism

Respect prior studies and their purposes, but permit strong problematization when evidence warrants it. Critique assumptions, mechanisms, scope, or inference precisely; do not caricature earlier work to manufacture novelty.

### 5. Broaden the context decision

Foreground context when it changes construct meaning, mechanisms, boundaries, measurement validity, temporal dynamics, stakeholder consequences, or the transferability of theory—not only when it reverses a correlation. Background context only after explaining why it does not carry theoretical weight.

### 6. Separate structure from epistemology

Use exemplars to learn navigation, disclosure, and reader expectations. Do not copy their claim sequence when the current study uses a different paradigm, evidence type, or theoretical purpose.

### 7. Link every theoretical promise to empirical leverage

Map each promised mechanism, moderator, process, or boundary to evidence capable of distinguishing it from plausible rivals. A sophisticated analysis cannot rescue weak construct validity, temporal mismatch, or an unidentified causal claim.

### 8. Bound claims and contribution counts

Do not require three or four contributions. Prefer the smallest coherent set that the evidence can support. Keep discussion implications tied to the front-end promise, and separate supported implications from post hoc possibilities.

## Promise-delivery trace

For a full manuscript, build this matrix:

| Front-end promise | Focal conversation | Mechanism/explanation | Evidence or design test | Finding | Discussion delivery | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Classify each row as delivered, partly delivered, unsupported, contradicted, or untested.

## Maximum-claim rule

Identify the weakest link in the argument chain. State the strongest wording the current evidence permits, then state what additional theory, analysis, data, or design would be required for the stronger wording.

Examples of distinctions to preserve:

- description vs. association;
- association vs. prediction;
- prediction vs. causation;
- static relationship vs. temporal process;
- measured mechanism vs. mechanism-consistent pattern;
- sample-specific result vs. broader theoretical boundary.
